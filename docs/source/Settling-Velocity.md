In a clear fluid at rest, the fall velocity of a single sediment particl with a size up to 100 \mu m, can be calculated by a balance of drag forces and the gravitational force. The force of viscosity and the gravitational force for a spherical particle with uniform density are given in the following equations:

$$F_d=6\pi\ \nu\ w_s\ \frac{D}{2}$$

with
$F_d$ the stokes drag force $(kg\ m\ s^{-2})$  
$D$ the particle diameter $(m)$  
$w$ the velocity $(m\ s^{-1})$  

$$F_g=\frac{\left(\rho_s-\ \rho_w\right)g4}{3}\pi\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2$$

with  
$F_g$ the gravitational force $(kg\ m\ s^{-2})$  
$\rho_s$ the density of sediment $(kg\ m^{-3})$  
$\rho_w$ the density of water $(kg\ m^{-3})$  

The terminal velocity is than equal to:

$$w_s=\frac{2}{18}\frac{\left(\rho_s-\ \rho_w\right)}{\nu}g\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2$$

with  
$w_s$ the settling velocity (terminal velocity of the particle) $(m\ s^{-1})$  

When particles reach larger grain diameters, the assumptions that are needed for the stokes range terminal velocity do no longer hold since the drag force behaves differently at those scales. For these particles, an equation by Zanke (1977) can be used.

$$w_s=10\ \frac{\nu^2}{D}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{0.01\left(\frac{\left(\rho_s-\ \rho_w\right)}{\rho_w}gD^3\right)}{\nu}}-1\right)$$
